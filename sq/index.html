Where it stands

Parked on purpose. The engine is fine, the battery is not. On October 30 I took the project to a point where every piece was verified and stopped there. Not abandoned: parked, with a written list of what to do when I come back. The misfires are not getting worse. The warm misfire rate has sat at about 8 to 9 per 10,000 firings for ten weeks straight; the weekly card would say "keep an eye on it" at 1.25 times the four-week baseline and flag it at 1.5 times, and it has done neither. Before the plugs it ran at 12. The battery is the open problem: the weekly health card has flagged weak starts, and the Pi rebooting all night in the parked car is what drains it.

What changed at the gate, and since:

  • Raw bytes are kept now. The recorder writes every response from the engine computer as bytes, next to each drive's CSV, and a tool rebuilds the CSV from them with any channel map. A decoder fix can be re-run over old drives. This went live on the Pi on November 1, and the first drive after it rebuilt byte-identical: 1,451 rows, 55 columns, zero differences.
  • Every drive says where it came from. Clock type, UTC offset, decoder and channel-map version are stored with each drive. Older drives were back-filled from their own data.
  • Pi power, measured. A USB meter on the bench, a million samples over three hours: 2.6 W idle after trimming the boot config and switching off services the car never needed. Turning the Wi-Fi radio off while parked takes it to 2.35 W, about 9% less, and nothing else in software moved it more than 0.04 W. So the Pi is set to drop Wi-Fi once a drive is pushed home and to poll a sleeping car once a minute instead of every few seconds. That is on the Pi now, and still being proven on real drives before I call it done. A Pi 4 bottoms out near 2.3 W. Below that is a hardware cutoff, which is still the plan.
  • A live view on the phone. The Pi can serve a read-only live wire to the phone, and an in-car dashboard companion exists as a test build. Neither is in the car yet.

What's open:

  • Saving a drive in the app fails on a plain-http page, because the browser only offers the hashing it needs over https. The fix is drafted.
  • The corpus dedupe rests on a 32-bit hash. A collision would silently skip a real drive. It hasn't happened, but it could.
  • A drive's "captured at" means when the file was parsed, not when the car was driven.
  • Drive history is blind to sessions: a 42% heat-soak cluster hides inside a 29% overall rate, and they should be two different numbers.
  • Per-cylinder work: capture misfire and knock per cylinder end to end, then two rules on top. That still waits on one hot evening at the car with a proper per-cylinder read.

How it started

The thermostat went. After that I wanted to see the next problem coming, not find out about it after.

So on June 27, 2026 I started on the couch, on my phone: a GitHub repo, a page on what this had to become, a roadmap and a decision log, all typed into GitHub's web editor. It had three jobs. See everything coming. Catch problems early. And build an engine database clean enough to train on. Everything below is that, one step at a time.

How the data gets from the car to this page

Two ways into the car got built, and only one of them is in the car today. The tune came with a tuning cable and a Windows app I didn't want to depend on, so I reverse-engineered the cable: request and response over a serial link, a framed message with a 16-bit checksum, a handshake that opens "measurement mode," then about five reads a second of engine-computer variables. That work produced the channel map, which is the whole prize: which address in the engine computer's memory holds which value, at what scale. It still works with a laptop plugged in, and it is not how the car logs. The full write-up, including the bug that made a million readings silently wrong, is in obd-cable-protocol-re.

Since July 19, 2026 the logging path is the car's own internal network, the CAN bus. A small USB-to-CAN adapter sits on three wires at the diagnostic port and asks for the same channels the cable did, with no vendor hardware in the chain: 58 of them come back about two and a half times a second. They are not generic OBD values. They come from the engine computer's own memory: per-cylinder misfire counters, knock correction per cylinder, oil and transmission temperatures, boost, lambda (how rich or lean the engine is running), voltage at the module. The wiring, and why one pin at the port is deliberately left unconnected, is on the logger tab.

A Raspberry Pi 4 in the passenger footwell runs the logger on every drive. Each drive is saved on the Pi first. When the car is parked at home, the Pi joins the home WiFi and sends everything over to jarvis, the home server, which keeps the master copy. Away from home it holds on to the data and tries again every ten minutes. Every row goes through one normalizer, a step that checks and renames it, so nothing reaches the database by a side door.

It rides in a printed box with no fan, which took two attempts and 121,072 temperature samples to earn. That story, and the box itself taken apart from the lid screws down to the base, is on the logger tab.

What the data taught me

  • Misfires are a rate, not a count. The counter never resets on its own, so only rates per 10,000 firings compared week to week mean anything. Day-to-day noise is about five times what a naive model predicts. Single days lie.
  • The cylinder that stands out keeps changing, and that is the finding. Through September it was cylinder 3, at about twice the rate of the others, and the excess was the same at every load. New plugs cut the warm misfire rate across all six by 38% and made what was left depend on load. By early November cylinder 3 had drifted back to the pack and cylinder 6 was the one flagged. On the current card both are above the line, cylinder 6 at 2.1× its siblings and cylinder 3 at 1.6×. One week is a symptom. Only the run of weeks is a diagnosis.
  • Nearly 40% of all misfire counts land in the second after an upshift, which is under 10% of driving time. That is not one cylinder's fault. It is shared, with cylinders 3, 4 and 6 about twice 1, 2 and 5 inside that window.
  • Cylinder 4 dislikes cold oil. With the coolant warm but the oil under 50 °C it misfires at nearly three times its warm-oil rate. Nothing else does that.
  • The battery problem was my logger. On long parks the Pi fell into boot storms, up to 128 reboots in a day, each one a drain. The fix is a hardware low-voltage cutoff, not more software.
  • High charging voltage is normal here. A 15.3 V median looked like a sick battery until I learned this car recovers energy when it slows down and charges harder while it does. Learn the car's normal before judging a number.
  • Group by engine load before comparing two stretches of time, and put a drive in the week it started, not row by row. Both bit me before I wrote them down.

The weekly health card

Once a week a job compares the week to the week before and to a four-week baseline and writes one message: the verdict in words first, then what to do, then the evidence with its threshold in the same sentence. "Oil peaked at 104 °C, fine, worry starts at 130." No channel names, no units nobody asked about. The current card is here, as a static snapshot. It is deliberately not a live feed: a live feed of a car tells the world when it is and isn't home.

The idea

Raw vehicle data has little value; understanding has a lot. Instead of asking the owner to understand the car, SQ understands the car for the owner. The goal is a screen that says "Your vehicle is healthy. Nothing unusual has changed since your last drive." instead of a page of voltages.

Data layer
Collects and normalizes. Makes no decisions.
Rules engine
Turns data into facts. Never decides what the owner sees.
Vehicle brain
Combines state, history, baselines and rules into understanding. Every car has its own normal, and the brain learns it.
Interface
Shows only what the brain prepared. No parsing, no thresholds, no severity logic in the screens.

History is a living medical record for the car, not a log. Further out: a Vehicle Passport, so a used-car buyer can see how a car actually lived. AI comes last, reasoning from years of structured knowledge rather than guessing. Architecture came before screens on purpose: features are temporary, architecture is permanent.

The app is phone-first and opens on 35 real drives. Try the demo.

Problems I hit

  • Populated is not correct. Three confident claims rested on a dead fuel-trim channel, a boost setpoint read as measured boost, and a rail-pressure column mistaken for boost. 14 channels now carry suspect flags.
  • Clocks with no label. Logger time was local and unlabelled, so drives landed in the wrong week. Every drive now records its clock, offset, decoder and channel-map version.
  • No raw bytes kept. A decoder fix could not be re-applied to old drives. Now every drive keeps a copy of its raw bytes next to it, and a tool can rebuild the drive from them.
  • A silent cable. No vendor app, and the serial link stayed silent at every baud rate. It turned out to need a handshake and an open command, then read engine-computer RAM, not OBD. About 5 Hz is the ceiling; poll faster and it returns nothing.
  • A test that stopped answering. The planned coil swap for cylinder 3 lost its decision rule once cylinders 4 and 6 caught up to it. It is re-planned around light-load counts outside shift windows.
  • Remote access went dark. The Pi wedged while parked: the port answered, the shell never did. It came back, and the deploy that had been waiting on it went through; the real bug was in my deploy script, which shipped only the first file in its list.

Plus the boot storms and the signed-channel bug above.

Rules I keep

  • Read only. My tools never flash or write to the car.
  • One normalizer. Nothing bypasses it.
  • Rates with baselines, binned by load, before blaming any cylinder.
  • Trust the physical part in your hand, not the listing photo.
  • Never delete an assertion to make a model render.
