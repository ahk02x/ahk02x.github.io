How the data gets from the car to this page

The car came with a diagnostic cable whose Windows app I didn't want to depend on, so I reverse-engineered the cable. The protocol is request and response over a serial link: a framed message with a 16-bit checksum, a handshake that opens "measurement mode," then about five reads a second of 53 engine-computer variables. These are not generic OBD values. They come straight from the engine computer's own memory: per-cylinder misfire counters, knock correction per cylinder, oil and transmission temperatures, boost, lambda (how rich or lean the engine is running), voltage at the module. The full write-up, including the bug that made a million readings silently wrong, is in obd-cable-protocol-re.

A Raspberry Pi 4 in the passenger footwell runs the logger on every drive. Each drive is saved on the Pi first. When the car is parked at home, the Pi joins the home WiFi and sends everything over to jarvis, the home server, which keeps the master copy. Away from home it holds on to the data and tries again every ten minutes. Every row goes through one normalizer, a step that checks and renames it, so nothing reaches the database by a side door.

The case is fanless by evidence, not by hope: 121,072 temperature samples over two weeks, a maximum of 67.7 °C on the chip, zero throttling. The first case failed on measurement, not material. I left 1.2 mm of clearance for a board edge that sticks out 1.2 mm. That is zero clearance, and the hold-down legs snapped. The second is written in OpenSCAD with fit assertions that caught four real collisions before anything was printed. Rule since then: when adding a port, model the connector, not just the board.

What the data taught me

  • Misfires are a rate, not a count. The counter never resets on its own, so only rates per 10,000 firings compared week to week mean anything. Day-to-day noise is about five times what a naive model predicts. Single days lie.
  • Cylinder 3 misfires about twice as often as the others, and the story changed. Before the new plugs the excess was the same at every load. The plugs cut the warm misfire rate across all six cylinders by about a third and made what was left depend on load. Same symptom, different shape, different suspect.
  • Nearly 40% of all misfire counts land in the second after an upshift, which is under 10% of driving time. That is not one cylinder's fault. It is shared, with cylinders 3, 4 and 6 about twice 1, 2 and 5 inside that window.
  • Cylinder 4 dislikes cold oil. With the coolant warm but the oil under 50 °C it misfires at nearly three times its warm-oil rate. Nothing else does that.
  • The battery problem was my logger. On long parks the Pi fell into boot storms, up to 128 reboots in a day, each one a drain. The fix is a hardware low-voltage cutoff, not more software.
  • High charging voltage is normal here. A 15.3 V median looked like a sick battery until I learned this car recovers energy when it slows down and charges harder while it does. Learn the car's normal before judging a number.
  • Group by engine load before comparing two stretches of time, and put a drive in the week it started, not row by row. Both bit me before I wrote them down.

The weekly health card

Every Sunday a job compares the week to the week before and to a four-week baseline and writes one message: the verdict in words first, then what to do, then the evidence with its threshold in the same sentence. "Oil peaked at 104 °C, fine, worry starts at 130." No channel names, no units nobody asked about. The current card is here, as a static snapshot. It is deliberately not a live feed: a live feed of a car tells the world when it is and isn't home.

The idea

Raw vehicle data has little value; understanding has a lot. Instead of asking the owner to understand the car, SQ understands the car for the owner. The goal is a screen that says "Your vehicle is healthy. Nothing unusual has changed since your last drive." instead of a page of voltages.

Data layer
Collects and normalizes. Makes no decisions.
Rules engine
Turns data into facts. Never decides what the owner sees.
Vehicle brain
Combines state, history, baselines and rules into understanding. Every car has its own normal, and the brain learns it.
Interface
Shows only what the brain prepared. No parsing, no thresholds, no severity logic in the screens.

History is a living medical record for the car, not a log. Further out: a Vehicle Passport, so a used-car buyer can see how a car actually lived. AI comes last, reasoning from years of structured knowledge rather than guessing. Architecture came before screens on purpose: features are temporary, architecture is permanent.

Problems I hit

  • Populated is not correct. Three confident claims rested on a dead fuel-trim channel, a boost setpoint read as measured boost, and a rail-pressure column mistaken for boost. 14 channels now carry suspect flags.
  • Clocks with no label. Logger time was local and unlabelled, so drives landed in the wrong week. Every drive now records its clock, offset, decoder and channel-map version.
  • No raw bytes kept. A decoder fix could not be re-applied to old drives. Now every drive keeps a copy of its raw bytes next to it, and a tool can rebuild the drive from them.
  • A silent cable. No vendor app, and the serial link stayed silent at every baud rate. It turned out to need a handshake and an open command, then read engine-computer RAM, not OBD. About 5 Hz is the ceiling; poll faster and it returns nothing.
  • A test that stopped answering. The planned coil swap for cylinder 3 lost its decision rule once cylinders 4 and 6 caught up to it. It is re-planned around light-load counts outside shift windows.
  • Remote access went dark. The Pi wedged while parked: the port answered, the shell never did. The next deploy waits on it.

Plus the boot storms and the signed-channel bug above.

Rules I keep

  • Read only. My tools never flash or write to the car.
  • One normalizer. Nothing bypasses it.
  • Rates with baselines, binned by load, before blaming any cylinder.
  • Trust the physical part in your hand, not the listing photo.
  • Never delete an assertion to make a model render.
