Amir Khademi / About me

Hi, I'm Amir.

I like knowing how things work. Especially the things I use every day.

That includes the car I drive, the servers in my office, and the tools I build when something keeps annoying me. A small question has a habit of turning into a project.

I'm a platform and security engineer on a payments platform. Most of my hard tickets turn out to be a certificate, a login or a firewall. Away from work, you'll usually find me somewhere between a terminal, the car and a 3D printer.

Something
of my own.

At work, I help keep systems running that people depend on. At home, I want to build things I can keep, understand and change myself. That's why there's a lab in my office, why I save my project history, and why I keep coming back to local software.

One early attempt was a little assistant I called Jarvis. In a Windows terminal, I taught it to say hello. Then I tried to get it to remember things. It was rough, and I kept going back with the next error and the next idea.

That still feels like the right way to learn: pick something I actually want, get a small part working, and keep asking questions. The long-term ambition is a personal assistant running on hardware I control, with a memory I can take with me.

Some stories.

I taught myself my car's diagnostic cable.

The tuning cable only talked to a Windows app. I wanted to understand what my own car was doing, so I worked out the framing and checksum byte by byte and built a read-only logger. Hundreds of drives later, it showed a pattern I hadn't seen before: misfires clustered just after upshifts.

That became SQ. The car is still my daily driver; it just gives me a lot more to think about now.

Sometimes it's just a part that won't fit.

A washer-fluid adapter sent me through round after round of small changes: the lip, the neck, the filter tabs. The notches needed to stay square. It sounds tiny until you're holding the part and it still isn't right. I enjoy that connection between an idea on a screen and something I can actually use.

I want to see the evidence.

A certificate parser led me from an unexpected memory cost to a report to the Go team, a public issue, and a small library called certguard. I like following a question far enough to make it reproducible. The write-up includes the measurements and the limits of what they show.

Where I've
worked.

Cloud infrastructure, telecom, financial services and now payments. More responsibility each time, and a little closer to the systems people depend on.

I started with an honours degree in software engineering. My first team lead role was a Java app that taught children Braille. Since then, the work has grown from looking after servers to carrying changes through production and helping teams recover when things go wrong.

01 / Foundations

Cloud & software

I started as a systems administrator on a large cloud platform, supporting hundreds of customers across Linux and Windows. I automated VMware tasks with PowerShell and Python, hardened more than 100 servers to a healthcare compliance standard, and worked on Cisco switches and ASA firewalls.

The automation cut the team's manual work by about a third. It made me want to look for the next repetitive job worth fixing.

Alongside the technical work, I was president of the intern community across two provinces: a few hundred interns, a calendar of social and career events, and site leads checking in each week. Keeping people connected and staying within budget was a different kind of systems problem.

02 / Scale

Telecom

I helped keep mobile provisioning flowing for millions of LTE and 5G subscribers. I wrote outage playbooks, built patch procedures for on-premises and cloud systems, and carried those changes through review.

The work also meant pursuing product defects with vendors and building a tool that took repetitive cleanup off the frontline network operations team. The platform portfolio maintained a 98% provisioning success rate.

The team won the company's Team of the Year award for a world-first launch with a major software partner. Launch day on a provisioning system is the one day nothing is allowed to queue.

03 / Responsibility

Financial services

This is the part I'm proudest of. I worked with the banks and credit unions on a financial services platform, owning the hub service between them. For each partner, I wrote the configuration, raised the change, and carried it through development, testing and production.

I ran major release migrations, wrote the automation that made them repeatable, and rotated certificates without dropping traffic. The hard tickets involved mTLS, SAML and OIDC federation, HSM signing, and site-to-site VPNs that only made sense once you found the cause.

In practice that meant Keycloak realms and clients built with Ansible, mTLS keystores and truststores redeployed without breaking a partner, and the yearly rotation of keys that never leave the hardware security module, with the risk written up before the change went in. Ansible did the heavy lifting, at volume. I was on call for all of it, and when something broke I wrote the postmortem.

That work spanned two data centres, with Nomad, Consul, Vault, Ansible, AWS and Bitbucket pipelines. In a regulated environment, a mistake can affect somebody's payment. A recovery plan is part of the job.

I also handled the staff side: Azure AD single sign-on, Intune and Autopilot, plus testing and rollout for an organisation-wide Windows 11 migration. Vulnerability remediation stayed at 90% within SLA.

04 / Now

Payments, platform & security

Today I'm on the platform engineering team of a payments platform at a large tech company. Before that I was on its network and security team, and most of what follows comes from there.

When a firewall vendor's credential leak hit the news, I owned our response: an inventory of every internet-facing device, a check of who had been notified, and a same-day answer to security leadership. I triaged the scanner's findings on the network gear down to one upgrade that closed them. I closed a security-operations escalation that turned out to be disaster-recovery test traffic looking like internal exploit scanning, and fixed an audit finding by shipping Linux audit privilege events into the SIEM.

Audits are a regular part of the job. I produced the quarterly access-review evidence across the firewalls, load balancers and switches, and the evidence for a payments-network audit.

I wrote the root-cause analysis for an IPsec tunnel outage and for a firewall failover incident, then pushed the redesign that came out of them: route-based tunnels with automatic failover. For an identity-platform migration I mapped the login flow nobody had written down, OIDC and SAML through a proxy, and wrote the break-glass and user-migration validation plan.

I wrote the network sections of a bank client's architecture document and fact-checked them against the device configs, and I contributed to the business case to replace the load-balancer and remote-access stack. I built a small Slack tool and took it through the company's app-approval process, which taught me more about enterprise security than the tool did. I use AI agents to cross-check documents, under redaction rules so no secret leaves the box.

I also screened and interviewed candidates for the team, and ran the knowledge transfer so after-hours support stopped depending on one person. Earlier at the same company I supported a commerce platform on OpenShift and Kubernetes for customers.
