01 / About

Hi, I'm Amir.

I like knowing how things work. Especially the things I use every day.

That includes the car I drive, the servers in my office, the dashboards I build so I can see exactly what my own setup is doing, and the tools I build when something keeps annoying me. A small question has a habit of turning into a project.

I'm a platform engineer. Away from work, you'll usually find me somewhere between a terminal, the car and a 3D printer.

Some stories

A small question, then a project.

01 / The cable

I taught myself my car's diagnostic cable.

The tuning cable only talked to a Windows app. I wanted to understand what my own car was doing, so I worked out the framing and checksum byte by byte and built a read-only logger. That gave me the channel map, and the car logs over its own internal bus now. Hundreds of drives later, it showed a pattern I hadn't seen before: nearly 40% of misfire counts land in the second after an upshift.

That became SQ. The car is still my daily driver; it just gives me a lot more to think about now.

02 / The part

Sometimes it's just a part that won't fit.

A washer-fluid adapter sent me through round after round of small changes: the lip, the neck, the filter tabs. The notches needed to stay square. It sounds tiny until you're holding the part and it still isn't right. I enjoy that connection between an idea on a screen and something I can actually use.

03 / The parser

I want to see the evidence.

A certificate parser led me from an unexpected memory cost to a report to the Go team, a public issue, and a small library called certguard. I like following a question far enough to make it reproducible. The write-up includes the measurements and the limits of what they show.

04 / The pager

I gave an AI a pager.

My home fleet had plenty of ways to alert me, but the coding agent started each session without that context. I built an alert board it had to answer, in writing, before moving on to my request. The hooks are currently off; the experiment taught me a lot about making an agent account for what it actually checked.

Why I build

Something of my own.

At work, I help keep systems running that people depend on. At home, I want to build things I can keep, understand and change myself.

That's why there's a lab in my office, why I save my project history, and why I keep coming back to local software.

One early attempt was a little assistant I called Jarvis. In a Windows terminal, I taught it to say hello. Then I tried to get it to remember things. It was rough, and I kept going back with the next error and the next idea.

That still feels like the right way to learn: pick something I actually want, get a small part working, and keep asking questions. The long-term ambition is a personal assistant running on hardware I control, with a memory I can take with me.

Experience

Where I've worked.

Seven years across cloud infrastructure, telecom and financial services. I've had the privilege of working on critical systems people rely on, in telecom and financial services. As time went on, the responsibility grew, and so did my experience.

I started with an honours degree in software engineering. My first team lead role was a Java app that taught children Braille. Since then, the work has grown from looking after servers to seeing changes through to production end to end: hunting down bugs, writing the infrastructure as code, and building the playbooks teams use when things go wrong.

01 / Foundations

Cloud & software

I started as a systems administrator on a large cloud platform, supporting hundreds of customers across Linux and Windows.

I automated VMware tasks with PowerShell and Python, hardened more than 100 servers to a healthcare compliance standard, and worked on Cisco switches and ASA firewalls.

The automation cut the team's manual work by about a third. It made me want to look for the next repetitive job worth fixing.

Alongside the technical work, I also ran the intern community, with social and career events and site leads checking in each week. Keeping people connected and staying within budget was a different kind of systems problem.

02 / Scale

Telecom

I was a senior systems analyst on the OSS platforms that provision the wireless network.

When someone activates a phone or changes a plan, that flow is what makes it happen, for millions of customers. I watched system health and KPIs, found the bottlenecks in LTE, 4G and 5G provisioning, and fixed them.

When things broke, I wrote the outage playbook the team used to recover faster. I wrote Methods of Procedure to patch on-premises and cloud systems and close vulnerabilities, and presented each change at change management.

I chased product defects with third-party vendors and tested the fixes with their engineers, built an automation tool that took customer-profile cleanup off the frontline NOC team, and led cross-functional projects to meet provisioning requirements across the whole OSS portfolio.

03 / Responsibility

Financial institutions

This is the part I'm proudest of.

For each change, I wrote the configuration, raised it, and carried it through development, testing and production.

I ran major release migrations, wrote the automation that made them repeatable, and rotated certificates without dropping traffic. The hard tickets involved mTLS, SAML and OIDC federation, HSM signing, and site-to-site VPNs that only made sense once you found the cause.

That work spanned two data centres. I turned business requirements into configuration as code, so every change was reviewed, repeatable and shipped through a pipeline instead of by hand.

I also carried L2/L3 production support on an on-call rotation for SLA-bound systems: responding to critical incidents, finding the root cause, writing the postmortem, and patching under pressure when it couldn't wait. Where the same manual step kept coming back, I wrote the Bash or Python to take it away.

I also handled Microsoft 365, Azure AD single sign-on, Intune and Autopilot, plus testing and rollout for an organisation-wide Windows 11 migration.
