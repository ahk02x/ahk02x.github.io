I like knowing how things work. Especially the things I use every day.

That includes the car I drive, the servers in my office, and the tools I build when something keeps annoying me. A small question has a habit of turning into a project.

Something
of my own.

At work, I help keep systems running that people depend on. At home, I want to build things I can keep, understand and change myself. That's why there's a lab in my office, why I save my project history, and why I keep coming back to local software.

One early attempt was a little assistant I called Jarvis. In a Windows terminal, I taught it to say hello. Then I tried to get it to remember things. It was rough, and I kept going back with the next error and the next idea.

That still feels like the right way to learn: pick something I actually want, get a small part working, and keep asking questions. The long-term ambition is a personal assistant running on hardware I control, with a memory I can take with me.

Some stories.

I taught myself my car's diagnostic cable.

The tuning cable only talked to a Windows app. I wanted to understand what my own car was doing, so I worked out the framing and checksum byte by byte and built a read-only logger. Hundreds of drives later, it showed a pattern I hadn't seen before: misfires clustered just after upshifts.

That became SQ. The car is still my daily driver; it just gives me a lot more to think about now.

Sometimes it's just a part that won't fit.

A washer-fluid adapter sent me through round after round of small changes: the lip, the neck, the filter tabs. The notches needed to stay square. It sounds tiny until you're holding the part and it still isn't right. I enjoy that connection between an idea on a screen and something I can actually use.

I want to see the evidence.

A certificate parser led me from an unexpected memory cost to a report to the Go team, a public issue, and a small library called certguard. I like following a question far enough to make it reproducible. The write-up includes the measurements and the limits of what they show.

I gave an AI a pager.

My home fleet had plenty of ways to alert me, but the coding agent started each session without that context. I built an alert board it had to answer, in writing, before moving on to my request. The hooks are currently off; the experiment taught me a lot about making an agent account for what it actually checked.

The wall stays red until I say so.

I turned a portrait monitor into an alarm for the lab. An alert stays visible until I acknowledge it. If the data feed dies, the display says it doesn't know. I wanted something I couldn't dismiss by forgetting to open another dashboard.

Where I've
worked.

Seven years across cloud infrastructure, telecom and financial systems. More responsibility each time, and a little closer to the systems people depend on.

I started with an honours degree in software engineering. My first team lead role was a Java app that taught children Braille. Since then, the work has grown from looking after servers to carrying changes through production and helping teams recover when things go wrong.

01 / Foundations

Cloud & software

I started as a systems administrator on a large cloud platform, supporting hundreds of customers across Linux and Windows. I automated VMware tasks with PowerShell and Python, hardened more than 100 servers to a healthcare compliance standard, and worked on Cisco switches and ASA firewalls.

The automation cut the team's manual work by about a third. It made me want to look for the next repetitive job worth fixing.

Alongside the technical work, I was president of the intern community: 600 interns, 40 social and career events, and 20 site leads checking in each week. Keeping people connected and staying within budget was a different kind of systems problem.

02 / Scale

Telecom

I helped keep mobile provisioning flowing for millions of LTE and 5G subscribers. I wrote outage playbooks, built patch procedures for on-premises and cloud systems, and carried those changes through review.

The work also meant pursuing product defects with vendors and building a tool that took repetitive cleanup off the frontline network operations team. The platform portfolio maintained a 98% provisioning success rate.

03 / Responsibility

Financial institutions

This is the part I'm proudest of. I worked with the banks and credit unions on the platform, owning the hub service between them. For each partner, I wrote the configuration, raised the change, and carried it through development, testing and production.

I ran major release migrations, wrote the automation that made them repeatable, and rotated certificates without dropping traffic. The hard tickets involved mTLS, SAML and OIDC federation, HSM signing, and site-to-site VPNs that only made sense once you found the cause.

That work spanned two data centres, with Nomad, Consul, Vault, Ansible, AWS and Bitbucket pipelines. In a regulated environment, a mistake can affect somebody's payment. A recovery plan is part of the job.

I also handled Microsoft 365, Azure AD single sign-on, Intune and Autopilot, plus testing and rollout for an organisation-wide Windows 11 migration. Vulnerability remediation stayed at 90% within SLA.
